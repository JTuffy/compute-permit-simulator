\begin{abstract}
\noindent Compute thresholds are emerging as a key mechanism for regulating advanced AI systems, with the EU AI Act and US Executive Order establishing requirements for training runs exceeding $10^{25}$ and $10^{26}$ FLOP respectively. However, the effectiveness of such regulations depends on enforcement, which operates under imperfect monitoring: regulators cannot directly observe compute usage and must rely on noisy signals from audits, hardware telemetry, and self-reporting. This paper develops a formal model of firm compliance decisions in compute permit markets and implements it in an agent-based simulation to compare three regulatory scenarios. We find that smart enforcement, combining moderate audit rates with upfront collateral and efficient permit pricing, achieves compliance levels comparable to strict enforcement at substantially lower regulatory cost. The key mechanism is incentive alignment: collateral requirements make compliance economically rational even when detection probability is moderate, while efficient permit pricing reduces the gain from evasion. Our analysis supports tolerance margins of 15-20\% to account for measurement uncertainty, scaled collateral based on firm size, and investment in monitoring infrastructure as a complement to audits. These findings offer guidance for policymakers designing compute governance regimes under realistic constraints on regulatory capacity.
\end{abstract}
